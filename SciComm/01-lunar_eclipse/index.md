This story begins in one of the largest library of the ancient world: the Royal Library of Nineveh. The library, commissioned by the last great king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, Ashurbanipal, contained more than 30,000 clay tablets, the medium on which epics, laws, omens, astronomical observations, and discoveries were recorded and preserved.

The library was built within the walls of Nineveh, the capital of the Empire, flourishing on the banks of the Tigris river in the middle of the VII century BC. High and long walls were protecting the city’s palaces, temples, and aqueducts. Inside the city, the palaces themselves were a testament to such magnificence. Their walls were adorned with bas-relief, depicting the devotion to the gods, the conquest of lands, and the glorification of the king.

But it is inside the library that this story continues. Some of the clay tablets there conserved begin with the phrase “Enuma Anu Enlil”, meaning “When the gods Anu and Enlil…”. These were no ordinary tablets: they contained around 7,000 celestial omens collected from across the Empire. They belonged to the Babylonian astronomical tradition and were studied and expanded by the Assyrians.

These clay tablets were used by the king’s circle of diviners to interpret divine messages revealed through celestial and terrestrial signs. Their interpretations of the omens were then sent to the king in the form of letters (also written on clay tablets, the “sheets of paper” of their time) for his consideration and further discussion.

In the case of bad omens, a series of apotropaic, protective rituals were performed. But in one case, even these were not enough. The divine verdict for a **lunar eclipse** occurring in a specific month, on a particular day, and at a particular time of night was a terrible one: “The king will die.”

And in the face of this omen, which no apotropaic ritual could counteract, the Assyrian king resorted to the *šar pūḫi*: the **ritual of the substitute king**. The king of the empire was removed from his position through a series of rituals that separated him from his royal identity. He was then sent away from the royal palace under a false identity, known as “the farmer”. A new king, chosen from among prisoners, ordinary citizens, magnates, or political enemies, was then enthroned and, with a series of rituals, metaphysically assumed the identity of the real king. After pretending to rule for 100 days, the substitute king was ritually killed or “helped” to die: the prophecy “The king will die” was fulfilled. “The farmer” could then reclaim his original royal identity.

However, lunar eclipses did not always bring trouble to kings. In ancient Greece this celestial event, while sometimes still being related to omens, offered an observational clue to understand the shape of our planet. At the time, thinkers without telescopes or modern instruments, were trying understand the cosmos through observation, geometry, and philosophical reasoning. And it was indeed Aristotle, one of the most influential philosophers, that around 350 B.C. wrote a book titled “On the Heavens”. In this treatise, he presented an elegant argument for Earth's spherical shape: no matter when or where a lunar eclipse is observed, the edge of Earth's shadow is always curved. Only a sphere, he concluded, could cast a such a shadow. Or, in a translation of his own words: “How else would eclipses of the moon show [Earth’s] segments shaped as we see them? […] In eclipses the outline is always curved: and, since it is the interposition of the earth that makes the eclipse, the form of this line will be caused by the form of the earth's surface, which is therefore spherical.”

And it is by looking at the slowly advancing shadow of the Earth on the pale lunar surface that at some point, in the case of totality, the Moon eventually becomes completely engulfed in the shadow. But, instead of disappearing into the dark, the Moon remains visible, usually wearing a new copper-red color.

The reason for this mysterious glow remained unknown for centuries, until Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), a key figure in the history of astronomy, proposed an explanation. Most of the Sun's light is blocked by Earth, but a small fraction passes through our planet's atmosphere, where it is filtered and bent towards the Moon. As the atmosphere scatters away much of the blue light, the remaining light is predominantly red and orange, illuminating the lunar surface even during a total eclipse.

Although this explanation had been known for centuries, it did not convince everyone. In 1877, Abbé F. Raillard proposed that the Moon's reddish color during a total eclipse was due to its “self-luminosity”, meaning the thermal radiation emitted by its surface, much like a piece of metal glowing in a furnace. His idea was that, after being exposed to the Sun's rays for several days, the lunar surface would become so hot that it would emit visible light of its own. Although interesting, this hypothesis was disproved that same year and atmospheric refraction was reaffirmed as the correct explanation. Indeed, even the hottest part of the Moon emits radiation, but in the infrared region and our eyes cannot see it.

But what is the actual temperature of the Moon? Thanks to modern observations, we now know that it varies a lot, reaching extremes far beyond those experienced on Earth. One of the reason is that one day on the Moon lasts roughly 29.5 Earth days, meaning that a given part of its surface can be exposed to sunlight for almost two weeks. In addition, the Moon has no atmosphere to redistribute heat or help retain it once the surface is no longer illuminated by the Sun. For these reasons, daytime temperatures can reach 125°C (about 400 K), while nighttime temperatures can be as low as −173°C (100 K). In some permanently shadowed regions near the lunar poles, temperatures can even drop below −233°C (40 K).

Interestingly, lunar eclipses also cause rapid changes in the temperature of the lunar Moon surface. These occur over a timescale of just a few hours, much shorter than the lunar day. By analyzing these temperature variations, scientists can better understand the physical properties of the material that makes up the surface.

It is with last piece of information that this story finishes, with the hope that now, whenever you will look to a lunar eclipse, you will be aware how this celestial event has frightened, fascinated, and inspired people over time. This connects us to all those who watched the same spectacle long before us.
